What you're building

The whole nave: ten bays, 60 m from the crossing to the west front. Round piers carry the arcade. A second row of piers divides the two aisles on each side, and a gallery runs over the inner one. Six-part vaults cover each pair of bays, 12 m across. At every bay line a flying buttress leaps both aisles in one flight to a buttress pier topped with a pinnacle. Then the oak roof frame.

Then the west front, 43.5 m wide: two square towers 69 m tall over the ends of the aisles, three portals, the Gallery of Kings, the rose 9.6 m across behind the Gallery of the Virgin, the grand gallery, and Emmanuel, the great bell, in the south tower. Between the towers the last bay of the nave gets a four-part vault.

Then the transept, 48 m from end to end: each arm with a six-part vault and a short end bay, a portal, a row of windows, a rose 13.1 m across and a gable. Over the crossing, on four piers, Viollet-le-Duc's spire of oak and lead, 96 m to the top of its cross: the one that fell in the fire of 2019, rebuilt to the same design.

Simplified: the vault's crown is about 35 m up here (33 m in the real nave); the chapels built between the buttresses in 1235 to 1245 are left out; every bay line carries the same share of the vault; the sculpture is plain shapes and the chimeras are left out; the transept's side walls are 3 m thick and carry the vault's push alone, where the real ones share it with the aisles and buttresses around them. The choir comes next.

The forces

The red line is the line of thrust through the vault's ribs, down the wall, along each flyer and through each buttress pier, worked out the same way as in the arch lab. They appear when the vault's centering is struck, the moment the vault starts carrying itself.

Color by load follows each piece's weight down to the ground and divides by the area it rests on. It ignores the sideways push, so it shows how load piles up, not exact stresses.
