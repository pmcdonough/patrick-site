# Roadmap

Where each project stands at site 0.88 and what comes next, in priority order. The product thinking behind Play Review is in the product brief (a Claude doc, https://claude.ai/code/artifact/c3d91c78-39ac-48bb-8d5b-bd518179cb7d); its substance is summarized here so the repo stands on its own.

## The idea, in one paragraph

A hockey engine whose decisions are tuned to public data is only a toy until it can be checked against what real players did. The path is: real game events in (done, from the NHL play-by-play and shift chart), the model's alternative for each decision out (done for shots versus passes and drives), then validation of those alternatives against a season of real decisions, then tracking data so the positions stop being inferred. Each step makes the Game plan lab more believable, because the lab is only as good as the decision model under it.

## Hockey Strategy Lab (sim 0.71)

1. Defense that allows the real number of chances. At sim 0.61, at the NHL settings, expected goals are 20 to 30% above the league (Boston 3.8, Montreal 4.0, league 3.1), mostly medium-danger looks: about 13 per team per game against 8; high-danger looks are at the league rate. Sim 0.58 fixed the biggest single cause: in man-on-man coverage nobody played the puck carrier from the inside (the man on him skated at the puck, and man coverage has no shot fronter), so a carrier who got by him had a clear path; on Boston's medium-danger shots against it the shooter's own defender was goal-side only 22% of the time. Playing the carrier from a stick length inside took Boston's expected goals from 6.0 to 4.3 and Montreal's coverage from the worst in the sim to even with zone. A tighter cushion on the men without the puck (6 ft or less) made it worse, not better. Sim 0.59 did the same for zone coverage's man on the puck (Montreal's expected goals against Boston 4.97 to 4.09, medium-danger 16.8 to 12.2). Measured in 0.61: 70 to 80% of medium- and high-danger shots come off a pass caught within 2.5 s, and when the pass leaves, the receiver's nearest defender is a median 12 to 14 ft away (in man coverage the man assigned to him is 18 to 21 ft away). The biggest single source, for both clubs, is a forward 10 to 20 ft out at even strength, about 5 a game each: passes into the gap between the defensemen at the posts and the center at the top of the slot. Sim 0.61 let man coverage sag further toward the net off a man far out (20 ft instead of 15: Boston 4.35 to 3.76 expected goals; 25 ft reached the league rate but cut Boston's shots on goal to 20 a game, so it went too far) and made zone coverage pick up a defenseman who comes down into the slot or the crease. Ruled out in 0.61: tighter man cushions again (6 and 9 ft, much worse even with the carrier played goal-side) and deeper slot coverage in zone (4.5 and 7 ft: no change for Montreal). Traced after sim 0.62 (5v5 shots from 10 to 20 ft off a pass, about 9 a game for both clubs together): they are rushes. Two seconds before the pass the receiver, usually the center driving the middle lane, is 55 to 60 ft from the goal line; one second before, 42 to 45; at the pass, 28 to 30, and he shoots from inside 20. Against zone coverage nobody is assigned to him until the pass (15 to 23% covered beforehand). So closing on the catch point cannot reach him; the gap is the back-check. Ruled out, each against the 0.62 reference: back-checking forwards picking up an attacking forward without the puck and getting goal-side of him, through the neutral zone and for 3 s after the entry (expected goals up for both clubs, 3.89 to 4.07 and 4.23 to 4.43); the same inside our zone only (Montreal 4.62); back-checking wingers coming back through the middle lanes instead of along the walls (4.00 and 4.46). Every version that takes a forward away from his zone spot to follow a man makes it worse, as tighter man coverage did. Next: whether these chances are the offense's doing rather than the defense's, starting with the read's pass value for a receiver in full stride (it already overprices passes, item 1 of Review this game), and how often a real rush ends in a shot from the low slot. Earlier findings: Measured in 0.56 (`docs/ENGINE.md`): about two thirds of the medium-danger shots come within 4 seconds of a zone entry, most after a clean breakout with the other team's defensemen already back, and on most of them no defender is closer to the net than the shooter. The first cause, a carrier skating through a defenseman, is fixed (angling, 0.56: high-danger chances down about 16%, offsides at the NHL settings from 9.2 to 6.2 a game). Ruled out, measured on top of angling: a wider neutral-zone gap (14 ft: expected goals within 1%), a wider gap read earlier (no better, more offsides), a bigger slot box (34 to 44 ft deep and 16 to 22 ft wide: slightly worse), and a slower puck carrier (the engine's 95% already matches the one published figure, about 5% slower with the puck in youth sprint tests). Not yet measured: holding the gap into our own zone for the first seconds of a rush (the first try passed two `--param` flags and only the last one applied, a `tools/lab.js` bug fixed in site 0.68). Also ruled out after site 0.68, each against the 0.56 reference: tighter slot coverage (goalSideFt 1.5, stickFt 6), sprinting sooner to a coverage spot (no effect: within 4 ft braking sets the speed anyway), longer sticks in passing lanes (6.5 ft: within noise), and the defender breaking on the catch point while a pass is in the air (medium-danger flat, expected goals slightly up). What these show: on the passes that become medium-danger shots the receiver is usually 12 ft or more from the nearest defender when the pass leaves, often with a defender assigned to him who has not closed, so nothing that acts at the catch or in the lane can reach him. Still open, and bigger: why coverage is that far off before the pass (the attackers' spots and drift pulling defenders out of the slot, or assignments switching), and whether the offensive systems put too many attackers in the medium-danger band. Start with a trace of one assigned defender over the five seconds before such a pass. `tierGoalScale` keeps goals realistic in the meantime (0.98 since sim 0.71, when attackers stopped standing in the crease; 1.02 in 0.62 to 0.70; 1.05 in 0.61; 0.92 in 0.60, when shots started hitting the net at the league's rate for their tier; 0.74 in 0.59, 0.68 in 0.58, 0.57 in 0.57, when a bug that let about three goals in ten in with no save roll was fixed) and is above its original 0.72 because both clubs' goalies are better than the league (each allowed about 10% fewer goals than expected), which the old shot accuracy hid; re-check it as the gap closes.
2. System effects that are still wrong (`docs/ENGINE.md`, System effects): crashing the net lowers expected goals for; aggression and the power-play systems do nothing measurable. Each has a `tools/lab.js --sweep` command to re-check it.
3. The build defaults (the faster Watch settings): balanced since sim 0.62, when acceleration came down from 2.3 to 1.8 (points 57% and 49%, goals 3.7 and 3.6 a game). At 2.3 Montreal's man coverage took three quarters of the points; at 1.5 Boston led 60 to 46 and games ran over 4 goals a team. The defaults are a choice for watching (`defaults.json`). Icings also run high at the build defaults (Boston about 9 a game), a side effect of the extra pressure in its own zone.
4. Long shifts. The coverage fixes of 0.58 to 0.61 kept teams pinned in their own zone, where nobody changes: shifts over two minutes went from about 1 a game at the build defaults (0.56) to 4, the longest past five minutes, and at the NHL settings to 8 a game. The cause was the man on a carrier on the wall holding a stick length inside him, so nobody took the puck; while pinned, the attackers carried it half the time (31% at 0.57). Sim 0.62 has him close on a carrier on the wall: about 2.4 a game over two minutes at the build defaults, none over three; 5.5 a game at the NHL settings. Still above 0.56. Ruled out: letting a tired unit clear at more pressure (icings tripled, and an icing team cannot change, so shifts got longer). Next: where the remaining long shifts end, and whether a pinned team gets enough loose pucks.
5. Blowouts. Under man-on-man the Canadiens score 10 in about 1.5% of games (a few a season across the NHL, about one team-game in a thousand): the coverage's extra chances with nothing to stop a rout (`docs/ENGINE.md`, Lopsided games). The coverage fix comes first; then a starter pulled after a bad night for the backup, and score effects (a trailing team pushes, a leading team sits back).
6. Overtime at three-on-three. Overtime is five minutes at full strength now; the lab's OTL counts would shift a little.
7. Validate system effects. The lab compares strategies inside the model; nothing says a 1-2-2 moves a real game the way it moves a simulated one. The honest first step is a season of real team results against the systems each club is known to run, which needs observed systems per club (item 5 in `docs/MODELING.md`).
8. A follow-the-puck camera for small screens, discussed and parked early (`docs/HISTORY.md` item 11). The pages themselves work on a phone; the rink is just small.
9. Tracking data. Each model has a specific use for it (`docs/MODELING.md`, What full tracking data would change); the first to swap would be coverage, since it is the engine's biggest error, then the expected-goal and save models.

## Game plan lab

Since 0.48 the lab is its own view with its own plans, and since 0.49 it reads top to bottom: the baseline, both plans side by side, then tests of the edited plans through the same games, all kept in the browser.

## Review a game (in the sim since 0.53, its own view since 0.68)

Play Review is moving into the sim: every shot of a finished game judged by the shooter's own read, with exact positions (`src/review.js`). Since 0.68 it is a view of its own, between Watch a game and the Game plan lab: a finished Watch game, or any lab game opened with Review it, which plays it on the page from its seed and plans, with a short list of recent games (the lab's kept in the browser). Next, in order:

1. Done in 0.55: What if he passed. It found that the read overprices passes: it values a pass as an instant one-timer, but played out the receiver often gets no shot away (4 to 40% of runs in the first games looked at), so many "better pass missed" shots come out about even. The carrier decides with the same read, so this is also an engine question: either receivers should one-time more often when the read says so, or the read should price the pass as it plays. Measure it across games before changing either.
2. Other decisions: entries (carry, chip, dump against who was back), passes that were picked off, and holds that ended in a turnover, each judged the same way.
3. Done in 0.69 and 0.70: Print or save as PDF, a lab report and a one-page report of the shot on screen, printed by the browser (the page makes no PDF itself).
4. A per-player view across games in a session. (The review for the lab's games is done in 0.57: any lab game opens on the Watch rink; since 0.68 it can be reviewed without watching it.)

## Play Review (0.10, the real-game prototype)

Since site 0.73 it has no card of its own on the home page; the sim's card links it as the first prototype, and the page stays where it was. The shot review inside the sim is where this work continues.

From the brief, in order:

1. Zone-entry review: the next play type after shots. Was the carry, chip or dump the best entry given who was back? Needs the same inference the shot review uses plus the entry events in the play-by-play.
2. Decision-agreement validation: score a season of real shots with the pass and drive alternatives and measure how often the model's preferred choice matches what the real player did, by situation. This is the number that says whether the model is worth listening to.
3. A game picker (any game ID through `tools/game_load.py`, the file name as a query parameter) and a per-player view (one skater's shots across games).
4. Observed systems per club for inference, replacing the hard-coded `OZ` table in `public/play-review/src/main.js`.
5. Tracking data. The replay and the inferred positions are placeholders for it; everything is built so real frames would replace them without changing the scoring.

## Keystone (0.4)

The nave (since 0.3) and the west front with its towers (since 0.4) of Notre-Dame in 3D, and the 2D arch lab, on one masonry solver. Next, in order: the transept, crossing and spire; the choir and apse with its long flyers; then (`docs/KEYSTONE.md`):

1. A treadwheel crane lifting each stone.
2. The cut face drawn as a solid section, with the 2D thrust diagram on it.
3. Real rigid-body collapse when a piece is removed; statics still decides whether it fails.
4. Lab experiments in 3D: remove a flyer, thin a wall, strike the centering early.
5. The Florence dome: drum, ribs, herringbone courses, two shells, chains, lantern; hoop forces in the solver.
6. Shareable Lab results by link.
